How is the derived base worked out?

Rising air cools about 5.4 °F for every 1,000 ft it climbs, while its dew point drops only about 1 °F. Where the two meet, the moisture condenses: that’s the cloudbase. So a station’s temperature and dew point tell how high its air would have to rise to make cloud: about 230 ft for every degree between them, added to the station’s own height. 70 °F with a 60 °F dew point at Kula Ag (3,162 ft) gives about 5,400 ft.

The page does this for about a dozen slope stations every 15 minutes once the upslope has started, and shows the middle value.

It can differ from the camera because the stations measure the air at the ground. Late in the day, or under cloud shade, that layer cools and moistens and no longer matches the air the thermals carry up, so the derived base reads low. Wet ground after dew or rain does the same in the morning. Before the upslope starts, the stations sit in the night’s drainage air, so nothing is shown. When the camera has a reading, it leads.
